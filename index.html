Spain

Oriol Vilanova

Los restos (The Remains)

Spanish Pavilion

Every wall of the pavilion is covered, edge to edge, with old postcards. There are about 20,000 of them, hung in a tight grid with no frames and no labels.

The artist

Vilanova has spent more than twenty years buying postcards at flea markets and second-hand shops. The collection itself is the art: what happens when you gather thousands of small, cheap, forgotten things and show them all at once.

Look for

  • Stand in the middle of a room first. From a distance the cards blur into bands of colour, like a mosaic or a woven fabric.
  • Then walk right up to a wall. The colour breaks into individual places: Alpine peaks, beaches, museums, hotels, holiday views.
  • Nothing is singled out. There's no “star” card and no explanation, and every card gets exactly the same space.

Ask yourself

Each of these was bought as a souvenir, posted to someone, kept for years, then thrown away. Which one would you have sent home?

Kenya

Wangechi Mutu

The End, Where All Began, EdEN, 2026

Central Pavilion · In Minor Keys · Video

A dim room retells the Garden of Eden with Mother Earth in place of God the Father. A low mound of earth shaped like a pregnant belly and breasts (MothersMound) rests on a platform. Nearby, a giant broom made of branches and human hair sweeps red earth and coffee grounds into circles on the floor, while a sunset and drifting lines of text are projected onto the curtains.

The artist

Mutu was born in Nairobi and works between Kenya and New York. She is known for creatures that are part woman, part animal, part plant, and for creation myths told from a woman's point of view. She often builds with soil, wood, hair and other natural materials.

Look for

  • The broom moves by itself. Watch the circles it draws in the earth, then rubs out and draws again.
  • The mound is real soil, so it reads as a body and a landscape at the same time.
  • Read the words drifting across the projected sunset.

Ask yourself

Why tell the story of the world's beginning with a broom, the most ordinary household tool?

1 item

Hungary

Endre Koronczi

pneuma cosmic

Hungarian Pavilion

The whole pavilion is about air: breath, sighs, wind and the draughts that move through buildings. In Both Feet Above the Ground, Koronczi walked for a year searching for “the most important sigh.” He caught it in a blown-glass object and carried it, sealed, in a frame on his back. The screens follow that walk through forest, snow and city streets at night.

The artist

Koronczi's project is a kind of invented science of airflow, mixing research that looks scientific with poetry and humour. Pneuma is the ancient Greek word for breath, and for the life force people once believed was carried in it. Elsewhere in the pavilion are a ventilation system salvaged from a Budapest science academy and a moving piece that “breathes.”

Look for

  • The frame on his back, and the care he takes carrying something that is basically invisible.
  • Pace: the pavilion is meant to be taken slowly, with a soundscape by composer Máté Balogh.
  • The glittering ceramic arch you walk through on the way in (see Around Venice).

Ask yourself

If you could seal up one breath or sigh from your own life, which moment would it come from?

USA

Alma Allen

Call Me the Breeze

United States Pavilion

Smooth, rounded sculptures in bronze, wood and white marble fill the pavilion. In the domed central room, a tall gold-coated form twists upward like a cactus, a flame or something still growing.

The artist

Allen is self-taught. He grew up in Utah, got his start selling small carvings from an ironing board on a New York street while homeless, and now works in Mexico. He calls every piece Not Yet Titled because he wants you, not him, to decide what it is.

Look for

  • Shapes that feel alive, like a worm, a bud, a bone or a knot, without being any one thing.
  • Surfaces: some bronzes are polished to a mirror shine, others are coloured with chemicals the way you'd use watercolour. The marble is the same Colorado stone as the Lincoln Memorial.
  • Outside: a headless sheep in the courtyard (his self-portrait as an outsider) and a bronze evil eye on the façade to ward off bad vibes.

Ask yourself

With no title to steer you, what does the gold sculpture remind you of?

2 items

Nordic Pavilion · Norway

Tori Wrånes

Human Lace

Nordic Pavilion · How Many Angels Can Dance on the Head of a Pin? · Video

Huge, headless figures in padded cream workwear, with dark bronze hands, crawl, bend over backwards and lock together through the pavilion. They look halfway through turning into birds.

The artist

Wrånes is a Norwegian artist known for troll-like creatures, costumes, performance and her own remarkable singing voice. Her work keeps blurring where a person ends and a creature begins.

Look for

  • The hands. They're realistic and heavy-looking, a sharp contrast with the soft, puffy suits.
  • Without heads, all the feeling comes from posture: straining, arching, resting.
  • The building: Sverre Fehn's 1962 pavilion has a roof of thin concrete fins, with trees growing up through it. The figures use it like a climbing frame.
  • Listen out for the foghorn that sounds every hour.

Ask yourself

Without faces to read, do the figures seem playful, struggling or something else?

4 items

Nordic Pavilion · Sweden

Klara Kristalova

Lust for Life

Nordic Pavilion · How Many Angels Can Dance on the Head of a Pin?

A fallen tree runs the length of the pavilion, and at one end it becomes a reclining woman with a pale face and long black hair spilling onto the floor. Small creatures in ceramic, bronze and wood live along the trunk: a fox, crows, little forest spirits and new shoots.

The artist

Kristalova was born in Czechoslovakia and lives in Sweden. Her glazed ceramic figures sit somewhere between human, animal and plant, like characters from a fairy tale that's both sweet and unsettling.

Look for

  • Walk the whole trunk and find the creatures one by one. The fox is easy to miss.
  • The jumps in scale: a woman many metres long, next to animals at life size.
  • The shiny glaze of the ceramics against the rough, dark bark.

Ask yourself

Is she sleeping, dying, or slowly turning into the forest?

Japan

Ei Arakawa-Nash

Grass Babies, Moon Babies

Japanese Pavilion

208 realistic baby dolls in mirrored sunglasses take over the pavilion. They climb scaffolding, cling to the stair rail, line up behind glass and sit under a canopy of hanging feeding bottles. You can pick one up and carry it for your whole visit, then change its nappy at the end and scan a code for a “diaper poem.”

The artist

Arakawa-Nash makes performances that only work when other people join in. The artist became a parent to twins in 2024, and this piece is about care: who does it, and who gets credit for it. Each doll's “birthday” is one of 57 dates from history about war, women's rights, queer history and work in Japan and across Asia.

Look for

  • Each doll weighs 5–6 kg, about the same as a four-month-old. Notice how holding one changes the way you move.
  • The sunglasses reflect you back, so you see yourself through the babies' eyes. They also hide the babies' faces from cameras.
  • Watch how other visitors behave once they're holding a “baby.”

Ask yourself

Did carrying a doll change how you looked at the art, or at the people around you?

Germany

Sung Tieu

Ruin

German Pavilion · with Henrike Naumann

The German pavilion was rebuilt by the Nazis in 1938. Tieu has covered its stern stone front with a full-size mosaic of an East Berlin housing block, graffiti and all, made from more than three million pieces of marble. It hides the word “GERMANIA” carved above the entrance.

The artist

Tieu was born in Vietnam and grew up in Berlin. The block she copied, in the Lichtenberg district, is where she lived with her mother in the 1990s, in housing for the Vietnamese contract workers brought to East Germany. Her work looks at migration, paperwork and how people get measured and sorted. Inside, Henrike Naumann, who died in February 2026, built rooms of flattened furniture about how far-right ideas grow out of everyday life.

Look for

  • How small the windows are next to the giant columns. Three people shared one room in that building.
  • From a distance it looks like grubby concrete. Up close it's polished marble, one of the most expensive materials there is.
  • Inside, an aluminium outline of her mother, and candy ladybirds with wooden centres where the chocolate should be.

Ask yourself

What happens to a building's meaning when you dress it up as a completely different one?

Australia

Khaled Sabsabi

khalil, 2026

Corderie · Video

You step inside a circle: a canvas about 40 metres long curves all the way around you. Projected onto it, shapes and colours drift, merge and split apart, with the ghostly negative of a crowd's faces, to slow, repeating music. Scent is part of the work too.

The artist

Sabsabi was born in Tripoli, Lebanon, and moved to Sydney in 1976 to escape the civil war. His immersive installations draw on Sufism, the mystical side of Islam, and on communities who know what it is to be displaced. Khalil is Arabic for a close friend or companion. He also represents Australia in the Giardini.

Look for

  • There's no front or back. It surrounds you, so where you stand changes what you see.
  • Faces appear and dissolve. Try to follow one person in the crowd.
  • Stay a few minutes and let the repetition work on you, rather than waiting for a story.

Ask yourself

Does being completely surrounded make you feel part of the crowd, or alone in it?

South Africa

Nicholas Hlobo

Corderie

Trumpets, horns and a saxophone lie scattered across a low platform, joined by long brass tubes that loop and coil over the floor like vines, or a drawing made in metal.

The artist

Hlobo is South African and usually gives his works Xhosa titles. He's known for pairing tough materials with delicate ones, such as rubber inner tubes with ribbon or leather with lace, to question ideas about masculinity and identity. His “silent wind orchestras” are brass instruments set up to sound with no one playing them.

Look for

  • The lines the tubes draw across the platform, and the shadows they throw.
  • The instruments point every which way, like a band that has scattered.

Ask yourself

What music do you imagine this orchestra would play?

El Salvador

Guadalupe Maravilla

ICE Age Disease Thrower

Corderie

Woven chairs shaped like bodies stand on a blue platform. They're wrapped in rope and fringe and studded with toys, beads, pompoms and found objects. Overhead hang large hammocks, each stitched with a line from a song.

The artist

Maravilla crossed from El Salvador into the US alone as a child in the 1980s and later survived cancer. He calls his sculptures “disease throwers”: healing objects that he often plays like instruments in gong sound baths. They're built from things gathered along the migration route through Central America.

Look for

  • The shapes come from the woven Acapulco chairs and hammocks found all over Central America. Here they turn into bodies.
  • Find the child's beanie with animal ears. It's modelled on one worn by a child held in US immigration detention.
  • Get close: there are hundreds of small objects, and some you'll recognise.

Ask yourself

Can an object heal? What would you put inside one?

USA

Carrie Schneider

First Living Woman

Corderie

Glossy photographic prints curl around rails in tall, layered towers. They're all cut from a single kilometre-long roll of photo paper, and they show the same woman's face again and again, in different colours.

The artist

Schneider, an American artist, built a camera the size of a room to make these. The face comes from Chris Marker's 1962 film La Jetée, which is told almost entirely in still photographs. For about eight seconds, the actress Hélène Châtelain opens her eyes and the film moves. Schneider's work deals with memory and with how women are shown on screen.

Look for

  • Faces and eyes come into view and disappear as you walk past.
  • Each print layers several exposures, so no two look alike.
  • The paper curls and bends. It's a physical object, not a flat picture.

Ask yourself

Why stretch eight seconds of film into a kilometre of paper?

1 item

USA

Nick Cave

Two Points in Time – At Once

Corderie, harbour and Giardino delle Vergini

Bronze figures cast from the artist's own body are placed like stops on a walk through the Arsenale and out into the garden. One sits on a chair with bare branches and birds where its head should be. In Siren, a column of bronze arms sprouts old gramophone horns. Another figure rushes forward under a huge bunch of flowers. Outside, Amalgam (Origin) towers over the harbour, and in the garden a cross-legged figure meditates under a great disc of bronze flowers.

The artist

Cave, who is based in Chicago, is famous for his Soundsuits: wearable sculptures he began in 1992 after the police beating of Rodney King. They cover the whole body, hiding race, gender and class. These bronzes carry that idea on, turning a Black man's body into a tree, a garden and a home for birds. The seven stops follow the stages of grief.

Look for

  • How the person disappears under the plants and birds.
  • It's all one dark colour, but the flowers and feathers are incredibly detailed. Get close.
  • Count the birds: Amalgam (Origin) holds about 50 species.
  • The poses: sitting, meditating, rushing forward. Each one is a different stage of grief.

Ask yourself

Is the nature growing over these figures protecting them or swallowing them?

Canada

Rajni Perera & Marigold Santos

Efflorescence / The Way We Wake, 2023

Corderie

A giant, terracotta-orange goddess props herself up on her arms. Her legs are detached and splayed out behind her, and her long breasts help hold her up. She wears an ornate mask and long black hair, and sits among “islands” of strange, spiky flowers and rocks. She's made of polymer clay, Styrofoam, synthetic hair and pearls.

The artists

Perera was born in Sri Lanka and Santos in the Philippines, and both live in Canada. Perera mixes science fiction with Sri Lankan folklore. Santos explores how moving between countries reshapes who you are. Together they invent goddesses and myths for people whose lives span more than one place.

Look for

  • The eyes behind the mask. She's looking back at you.
  • Her body is impossible, yet she looks powerful rather than broken.
  • Walk around her. The islands are laid out to give you several paths and views.

Ask yourself

What would a goddess look like for people who live between two countries?

Cameroon

Werewere Liking

Corderie

A painting of twisting figures in blues and ochres hangs on a tall wall faced in brick. Beside it, her poem Sous l'Égide des Pyramides (Under the Protection of the Pyramids, 2023) is printed large. It's about a shared African ancestry that reaches back to ancient Egypt, which she calls Kemet.

The artist

Liking was born in Cameroon and has lived for decades in Côte d'Ivoire, where she founded the Ki-Yi village, an arts community and school in Abidjan. She's a novelist, playwright, poet and painter who calls what she does “total art.” Her paintings return to suns, moons, trees, pregnant women and broken pots, and to healing, women's strength and migration.

Look for

  • The thick, rough paint, and the cool blues against the warm wall.
  • Read the poem out loud in your head, even in French. It repeats like a chant.

France

Kader Attia

Whisper of Traces

Corderie · Video

In a dark hall, thick ropes hang from the ceiling, wrapped in shards of broken mirror and bundles of dried herbs. Films play on the walls between them, and the mirrors scatter spots of light around the room. Further in, African ritual figures are shown alongside modern objects.

The artist

Attia grew up between France and Algeria. He's best known for the idea of “repair”: how people and cultures mend damage, especially the wounds left by colonialism. This piece starts from a Vietnamese shaman who said computer viruses are spirits, and asks why we treat technology and AI almost like gods.

Look for

  • The ropes: this long building is where the Venetian navy once made its rope.
  • Watch the reflected light shift when the ropes sway or someone walks past.
  • Smell the air. The dried herbs are meant to stir memories.

Ask yourself

Do you ever think of your phone or computer as having a mind of its own?

India

Himali Singh Soin & David Soin Tappeser

Corderie

A forest of long tapestries in deep blue, white and terracotta hangs from the high ceiling. The patterns look like symbols or writing, but they're actually sound waves: the shape of a piece of the duo's music, woven in silk.

The artists

Soin is an Indian poet and artist, and Tappeser is a German percussionist and composer. Together they make work about the polar regions and deep time, such as the fact that India and Antarctica were once joined. The space is meant for resting and listening. A master weaver, Gajam Govardhan, dyed every thread before weaving it.

Look for

  • Read a tapestry from top to bottom like a musical score.
  • Sit on the benches and listen.
  • Notice how the hangings sway slightly and change the room as you move through them.

Ask yourself

If you could see a sound, what shape would it be?

Chile

Alfredo Jaar

The End of the World, 2023–24

Corderie

You walk down a long, tall corridor flooded with red light. At the end, alone in a pool of light, sits a cube just 4 cm across. It's made from ten “critical minerals” pressed together: cobalt, rare earths, copper, tin, nickel, lithium, manganese, coltan, germanium and platinum. Panels on the wall explain where each one comes from.

The artist

Jaar is a Chilean artist based in New York. For forty years he has made work about the news we prefer not to look at, including wars, genocides and the people behind the headlines. He often works with light, empty space and very few objects.

Look for

  • How tiny the cube is compared with the room. The light, the length and the walk all build up to something you could hold in your hand.
  • The red light. Think about what red can mean: danger, heat, blood, stop.
  • Read the panels. These minerals are in phones, laptops, electric cars and weapons, and each one comes with conflict or environmental damage.

Ask yourself

You're probably carrying most of these minerals in your pocket right now. How does that feel, standing here?

South Africa

Kemang Wa Lehulere

I Bleach My Words for Your Comfort

Arsenale

An “allegorical library.” Green shelves hold more than 700 clay paving bricks painted chalkboard-black and shelved like books. Cast hands forming letters of the sign-language alphabet are scattered among them. The work is about censorship and the violence of controlled knowledge.

The artist

Wa Lehulere grew up in Gugulethu, a township in Cape Town. He often works with chalkboards, school desks and erasing, about what gets taught, what gets wiped away, and how memory survives under apartheid and after it.

Look for

  • The “books” are paving bricks, the kind that can build a road or be thrown in a protest.
  • The hands are spelling something out in a language you see rather than hear.
  • Chalkboard black: in theory you could write on these books, and wipe them clean again.

Ask yourself

What's the difference between a book you can't open and a book that's been banned?

USA

Laurie Anderson

Notebook

Arsenale

A room painted entirely black and covered floor to ceiling in Anderson's white drawings and handwriting, with sound playing throughout. It's like stepping inside a page of her notebook: dogs and crocodiles, a boy in overalls, news fragments in English and Italian, and half-told stories.

The artist

Anderson is an American musician, storyteller and artist who has spent fifty years mixing words, music and technology. Her work is often funny and sad at once. She has made chalk-drawing rooms before, and she often comes back to her late dog Lolabelle and to her Buddhist teacher, Mingyur Rinpoche.

Look for

  • Read the fragments, like “Try to practice how to feel sad without actually being sad” and “That's a really good dog! I like that dog!”
  • Find the sign-off near the corner: “I had a lot more to say but I ran out of paint.”
  • The brushstrokes are fast and loose, like notes jotted down in a hurry.

Ask yourself

Which line stuck with you, and why that one?

Botanical Biennale, 2026

Lebanon

Nabil Nahas

Don't Get Me Wrong

Lebanese Pavilion · Corderie

Twenty-six canvases hang side by side in a frieze 45 metres long, raised above eye level so you have to look up. Some are vivid, almost fluorescent landscapes with purple tree trunks, orange skies and fiery fields. Others are patterns built from thick layers of paint.

The artist

Nahas was born in Beirut and lives in New York. He's known for heavily textured paintings, some built from casts of starfish and repeating fractal patterns. More recently he has painted trees, including the cedars, olives and palms of Lebanon. Hanging the works high is, in his words, “a very Renaissance way of displaying art.”

Look for

  • Up close, the paint is thick and sculptural, not flat.
  • The colours don't match real life. Purple trunks and orange water make familiar places feel strange, even as if they're burning.
  • Walk the full length. It reads like a film strip, with each panel shifting from the last.

Ask yourself

Do these colours feel joyful to you, or like fire?

Luxembourg

Aline Bouvy

La Merde

Luxembourg Pavilion · Sale d'Armi

A film on a 4.5-metre screen follows a “trash creature,” a talking piece of poo, through a classroom, a bus and a bedroom, where other people's rules about bodies and behaviour keep pressing in on it. You watch from inside a half-circle of steel and mirrored glass that multiplies your own reflection.

The artist

Bouvy works with the body and with the things we're taught to find disgusting or shameful. She uses grossness and dark humour as a way to look at who gets treated as dirty, disruptive or out of place.

Look for

  • Your reflection. In the mirrors you become part of the audience being watched.
  • The moments when the creature's good behaviour finally cracks.
  • Her sculpture E.T. The Excremential, a cross between Spielberg's alien and the artist's own body.

Ask yourself

Why make something we're taught to hide into the hero of the story?

Morocco

Amina Agueznay

Asǝṭṭa

Moroccan Pavilion · Artiglierie

More than 200 large woven panels hang through the hall like walls of fabric, making passages, doorways and thresholds to walk through. They're hand-spun, naturally dyed wool, picked out with sequins, Murano glass and protective eye symbols. A big bed of white wool lets you lie down and watch weavers at their looms.

The artist

Agueznay works from Marrakech and makes her work alongside craftspeople. This piece involved 166 Moroccan artisans using weaving, embroidery, basketry and jewellery-making. Asǝṭṭa is the Amazigh (Berber) word for the loom and for weaving. It's Morocco's first pavilion at the Arsenale.

Look for

  • How the panels steer you through doorways and corridors, like moving through a house.
  • Get close and compare panels. Different hands left different patterns and stitches.
  • Lie on the wool bed and look up.

Ask yourself

Do you know who made the clothes you're wearing today?

India

Ranjani Shettar

Under the same sky

Indian Pavilion · Geographies of Distance: Remembering Home

Huge flowers, seed pods, leaves and curling tendrils, some bigger than a person, float overhead in ivory and gold. They're lit in a dark room, so they throw large, soft shadows across the floor.

The artist

Shettar, from southern India, shapes her sculptures by hand, usually from natural materials like wood, cloth, lacquer and plant dyes. They often hang in the air and play with light and shadow, looking like plants or sea creatures without copying any real one. This is India's first pavilion at the Biennale in seven years, and its theme is home.

Look for

  • The shadows on the floor are as much a part of the work as the objects.
  • Look from directly underneath, then from higher up if you can get there.
  • Behind the flowers, Asim Waqif's bamboo scaffold Chaal cuts through the space, like the building sites of India's fast-growing cities.

Ask yourself

Why might flowers and seeds make someone think of home?

India

Sumakshi Singh

Permanent Address

Indian Pavilion · Geographies of Distance: Remembering Home

A full-size house made only of white embroidered thread, with brick walls, latticed windows, a spiral staircase, a balcony and wrought-iron gates, and all of it see-through. It recreates the artist's family home in New Delhi, which has since been demolished.

The artist

Singh “draws” with thread instead of a pen, stitching delicate, lace-like outlines of places she has known. Her work is about memory, and about how the places we come from survive only as fragile outlines once they're gone.

Look for

  • You can see straight through the walls to the room behind. The house is there and not there at the same time.
  • Tiny details, like cracks in the plaster and the pattern of the bricks, are all stitched by hand.
  • The thread throws its own faint shadows on the floor.

Ask yourself

If you rebuilt your childhood home from memory, which details would you get right?

3 items

Uzbekistan

The Aural Sea

Uzbekistan Pavilion · group show

The Aral Sea was once one of the largest lakes in the world. Since the 1960s it has lost most of its water, because the rivers feeding it were diverted to grow cotton. Instead of facts and figures, this group show remembers it through myth and imagination.

The artists

Aygul Sarsen, from Karakalpakstan on the old shoreline, paints the sea as a woman or goddess: the painting here is Kutiw (Waiting), next to a wall of her sketches. Xin Liu wove plastic waste into delicate forms and sank them in tanks of enzyme solution, which slowly eats the plastic over the months of the show. A.A. Murakami's The Sun Sets in a Shell is a vast knitted textile whose pattern is coded from zebra mussels. The glowing orange painting is probably Jahongir Bobokulov's Aura.

Look for

  • In the tanks, check whether the woven plastic still looks whole. By the end of the Biennale it should be breaking apart.
  • Sarsen's woman covers her face with her hands, eyes wide open. Is she afraid, or waiting?

Ask yourself

How do you make art about something that isn't there anymore?

5 items

China

Nie Shichang

Kinetic Droplet Instrument (Di Dong Yi)

China Pavilion · Dream Stream · Video

In a dark room, a machine of stacked metal rings is built to recreate the ripples that spread out when a single drop of water hits a still surface.

The artist

It's part of China's pavilion Dream Stream, named after an 11th-century book of observations on science and nature by the scholar Shen Kuo. The pavilion mixes old Chinese ideas with robots and new technology. On the wall behind is Wang Dongling's giant swirling calligraphy, written partly with a robot arm.

Look for

  • Follow one ring at a time and watch the movement travel outward.
  • Compare the machine's rhythm with the brushstrokes of the calligraphy behind it.

Ask yourself

Can a machine capture something as fleeting as a ripple?

Iceland

Ásta Fanney Sigurðardóttir

Pocket Universe

Icelandic Pavilion · former shipyard, Castello · Video

The pavilion is spread through rooms of an old shipyard between the Giardini and the Arsenale, painted white and sky blue. A surreal film tells a creation story, with the artist as “Creature Zero” in a white wool costume. Around it are glowing orbs, yellow buoys, charms, handwritten poems, neon drawings on the brick walls and a sculpture made of Icelandic sheep's wool.

The artist

Sigurðardóttir is an Icelandic poet, musician and performance artist. Her work is playful and slightly mystical, mixing creation myths, wordplay and sound.

Look for

  • The blue. The whole space feels like being inside the sky or the sea.
  • The rippling sculpture on the floor, like a wave or a landscape seen from above.
  • The neon lines on the walls, like handwriting in light.

Ask yourself

If you invented your own creation myth, what would exist first?
